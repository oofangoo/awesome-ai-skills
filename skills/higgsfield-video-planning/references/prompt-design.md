# Prompts that specify an event

Write the positive action first. Identify subjects, starting positions, the initiating event, motion path and ending. Add the visual treatment, camera and sound that help the event read. Keep prohibitions focused on likely failures rather than burying the scene beneath a long negative list.

For each reference, state what it supplies: identity, opening composition, final pose, location, prop geometry, motion, camera or audio. Also identify conspicuous content that should not transfer. A style reference is not automatically a character reference, and a start frame can encode an unwanted body shape or pose that prose alone may not overcome.

Describe performance in visible terms: a hand tightens on a handle, eyes track the moving object, feet slip before the body rolls, a face changes after the failed attempt. Match the camera to the event: show contact and the resulting travel in a readable view; follow movement when losing the subject would hide the joke or explanation.

For physical comedy, use anticipation → trigger → displacement → consequence → reaction where that structure fits. State a source of force before a launch or bounce. Keep volume and anatomy consistent, contact brief and momentum understandable. Normal speed is a project choice; slow motion is appropriate when requested or when it serves the scene. Avoid making every episode a mutual headbutt merely because one example used it.

For products or factual scenes, replace comedic requirements with task-specific ones: readable materials and assembly order, a truthful demonstration, stable branding, or an evidence-supported explanation. Text fidelity and narrated facts need separate checks from attractive motion.

Use time ranges only as needed to control pacing. For a complicated shot, simplify competing actions or split the scene at a motivated boundary. More references and more instructions do not automatically produce greater control.
